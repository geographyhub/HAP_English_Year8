Part 1

Holmes and detective fiction

the Victorian era, 1837 to 1901

Who wrote the stories?

  • Arthur Conan Doyle wrote the Sherlock Holmes stories.
  • They were printed in The Strand Magazine and collected in The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes (1892).
  • Conan Doyle was a doctor. Like a doctor, Holmes studies small details to find the answer.

Why were detectives new and exciting?

  • The police force was new, and many people did not trust it.
  • Until 1842, the police only tried to prevent crime, not solve crimes that had already happened.
  • A murder took the police ten days to solve. After this, a detective force was set up to use clues.
  • Readers loved detective stories, and Holmes became hugely popular.

Sherlock Holmes

  • The protagonist (main character): a private detective who lives at 221B Baker Street, London.
  • He is meticulous: he pays great attention to detail.
  • He uses deduction: he reaches a conclusion by reasoning from clues.
  • He works for the love of solving problems, not for money.

Dr Watson

  • Holmes’s friend and assistant, and a doctor.
  • He is the narrator: he tells the stories in the first person (“I”).
  • He is often puzzled, so we learn the answer when he does. This builds suspense.

The conventions of a detective story

ConventionWhat it means
CrimeUsually a murder that seems impossible to explain.
VictimThe person harmed or killed.
DetectiveThe clever hero who solves the case.
CluesSmall details that help solve the crime.
Red herringA clue that misleads the reader (and sometimes the detective).
Suspects and motivePeople who might be guilty, and their reason for doing it.
The revealAt the end the detective explains how the crime was done.
Remember: The Speckled Band is a locked-room mystery: the crime happens in a room nobody seems able to get into, so it is hard to work out who did it and how.

Part 2

The Speckled Band: the story

The Adventure of the Speckled Band

  1. Early one morning, Helen Stoner arrives at Baker Street, shivering with fear. She asks Holmes for help.
  2. She lives at Stoke Moran with her violent stepfather, Dr Grimesby Roylott. He keeps a cheetah and a baboon, and lets gipsies camp on his land.
  3. Two years ago her twin sister, Julia, was about to be married. Julia heard a low whistle in the night.
  4. On a wild, stormy night, Julia screamed. Helen heard a whistle and a clanging sound. Julia died crying, “It was the band! The speckled band!”
  5. Now Helen is engaged too. Her bedroom is being repaired, so she has to sleep in Julia’s old room, and she has heard the same whistle.
  6. After Helen leaves, Roylott bursts in, threatens Holmes and bends the poker to show his strength.
  7. At Stoke Moran, Holmes finds a dummy bell-rope, a ventilator into Roylott’s room, a bed clamped to the floor, a saucer of milk and a safe.
  8. Holmes and Watson wait in the dark in Helen’s room. They hear a hiss, and Holmes lashes at the bell-rope with his cane.
  9. A terrible cry comes from Roylott’s room. He is dead, with a “speckled band” round his head: a swamp adder, the deadliest snake in India.
  10. Holmes explains: Roylott sent the snake through the ventilator and down the rope, then called it back with a whistle. He wanted to stop his stepdaughters marrying, so he could keep their money.
Red herrings: the gipsies (a “band” of gipsies) and the cheetah (which is speckled) make Holmes, and the reader, think of the wrong answer at first.

Part 3

The Speckled Band: characters and quotations

who they are, and what the words show

Helen Stoner

The client. She is frightened, alone and in danger, but brave enough to ask for help.

“It is fear, Mr. Holmes. It is terror.”

Helen to Holmes

Short sentences and repetition show how scared she is: it is not the cold that makes her shiver.

“her face all drawn and grey, with restless frightened eyes, like those of some hunted animal”

Watson describes Helen

Simile: she is like defenceless prey. The word hunted suggests someone is targeting her.

“her hair was shot with premature grey, and her expression was weary and haggard”

Watson describes Helen

She looks older than 30. Fear and stress have worn her out. The verb shot hints at violence.

Dr Grimesby Roylott

The stepfather and the villain. He is violent, greedy and clever.

“a fierce old bird of prey”

Watson describes Roylott

Animal imagery: he is compared to a bird of prey, which hunts weaker animals. He seems dangerous and predatory, the hunter to Helen’s “hunted animal”.

“See that you keep yourself out of my grip”

Roylott to Holmes, before he “snarled”

A direct threat. The verb snarled makes him sound like an angry animal.

“I am a dangerous man to fall foul of!”

Roylott to Holmes

He boasts about his violence, then bends a steel poker with his hands to prove it.

“Violence of temper approaching to mania”

Helen describes the Roylott men

His anger is close to madness. In India he beat his butler to death.

Julia Stoner

The first victim: innocent and helpless.

“her face blanched with terror, her hands groping for help”

Helen describes Julia’s death

The verbs blanched and groping show her terror and helplessness. We feel deep sympathy for her.

“Oh, my God! Helen! It was the band! The speckled band!”

Julia’s dying words

The most important clue. Exclamations show her panic. “Band” could mean the gipsies, but it means the snake.

Sherlock Holmes

Calm, clever and brave. He is never scared by Roylott.

“Your conversation is most entertaining”

Holmes to Roylott

Holmes stays calm and even jokes when Roylott threatens him. This shows he is in control.

“Violence does, in truth, recoil upon the violent”

Holmes, after Roylott dies

Roylott is killed by his own weapon. Holmes suggests this is justice.

Atmosphere on the night Julia died

“It was a wild night. The wind was howling outside, and the rain was beating and splashing against the windows.”

Helen remembers the night

Pathetic fallacy: the violent storm mirrors the violent crime. Howling sounds like an animal, or a person in pain. It foreshadows danger.

“a low whistle, such as my sister described, and a few moments later a clanging sound, as if a mass of metal had fallen”

Helen remembers the night

Sounds in the dark build tension. Both are clues: the whistle calls the snake back, and the clang is the safe door shutting.

Part 4

The Boscombe Valley Mystery: the story

The Boscombe Valley Mystery

  1. Holmes and Watson take the train to Boscombe Valley, in the countryside of Herefordshire.
  2. Charles McCarthy has been found dead beside Boscombe Pool, his head beaten in.
  3. His son, James McCarthy, is arrested. Witnesses saw them quarrel, and James was found with blood on his hand and sleeve.
  4. James says he heard his father call “Cooee!”, their usual signal. After the quarrel he walked away, heard a hideous outcry, and ran back.
  5. His father, dying, said something about “a rat”. James also saw something grey, like a coat, on the ground. When he looked again, it had gone.
  6. Everyone, including Inspector Lestrade, thinks James is guilty. But Alice Turner, who loves James, believes he is innocent.
  7. Holmes studies the footprints and finds a stone. He says the murderer is tall, left-handed, limps with his right leg, smokes Indian cigars and wears a grey cloak.
  8. Holmes works out that “a rat” was the end of “Ballarat”, a place in Australia.
  9. The murderer is John Turner, Alice’s father and the richest landowner in the valley. Long ago, in Australia, he was a robber called Black Jack of Ballarat.
  10. McCarthy had blackmailed Turner for twenty years. Turner killed him when McCarthy demanded that Alice marry James.
  11. Turner is dying. Holmes lets him go home, but takes a signed confession in case James needs it. James is set free.
Red herring: all the obvious evidence points at James, the son. Holmes says: “There is nothing more deceptive than an obvious fact.”

Part 5

The Boscombe Valley Mystery: characters and quotations

who they are, and what the words show

Charles McCarthy

The victim. Unlike Julia Stoner, he is not innocent: he is a cruel blackmailer.

“my father was a man of a very violent temper”

James describes his father

The victim is violent himself, so we feel less sympathy for him.

“He was not a popular man, being somewhat cold and forbidding in his manners”

James describes his father

The adjectives cold and forbidding make him seem unfriendly and hard.

“He was a devil incarnate.”

Turner describes McCarthy

A metaphor: McCarthy is evil in human form. This shows how much Turner suffered.

John Turner

The murderer, but presented with sympathy. He killed to protect his daughter.

“His grip has been upon me these twenty years, and he has blasted my life.”

Turner explains his motive

McCarthy controlled him through blackmail. Blasted suggests his life was destroyed.

“I am a dying man”

Turner to Holmes

He is ill and close to death, so we pity him even though he is a murderer.

James McCarthy

The son, wrongly accused. He keeps a secret: he is already married to a barmaid in Bristol, so he cannot marry Alice.

Sherlock Holmes

He looks past the “obvious” answer and shows mercy at the end.

“Circumstantial evidence is a very tricky thing”

Holmes to Watson

Evidence can seem to point one way, but look at it differently and it points somewhere else.

“There is nothing more deceptive than an obvious fact”

Holmes to Watson

The obvious suspect (James) is not the murderer.

“There, but for the grace of God, goes Sherlock Holmes.”

Holmes, after Turner leaves

Holmes feels sympathy: anyone could end up like Turner. He shows mercy, not just cleverness.

Part 6

Comparing the two stories

similarities and differences

Side by side

The Speckled BandThe Boscombe Valley Mystery
VictimJulia Stoner: innocent, helpless, sympatheticCharles McCarthy: violent blackmailer, unsympathetic
MurdererDr Roylott: a villain we fearJohn Turner: a murderer we pity
MotiveMoney: to keep his stepdaughters’ incomeBlackmail, and to protect his daughter
Red herringsThe gipsies and the cheetahThe obvious evidence against James
EndingThe murderer is killed by his own snakeHolmes lets the dying murderer go home

Similarities

  • Both have a murder that seems impossible to explain.
  • In both, the dying victim says strange words that are a clue (“the speckled band”, “a rat”).
  • Money plays a part in both crimes.
  • Holmes solves both by close observation and deduction.

Comparative connectives

  • To show a difference: however, whereas, in contrast, on the other hand.
  • To show a similarity: similarly, likewise, both, also.

Model comparison

Arguably, Conan Doyle presents Julia Stoner as a sympathetic victim: her “face blanched with terror” shows that she was frightened and helpless. In contrast, Charles McCarthy is presented as unsympathetic, because he was “cold and forbidding”. Perhaps Conan Doyle does this so that the reader feels pity for Turner, the murderer, instead.

Part 7

How to write about the extract

analysing a character with PEEL

The top-marks questions ask how the writer presents a character or an atmosphere. Use PEEL, as you do in class.

PPointAn idea about the character that matches your quote. Roylott is presented as violent and aggressive.
EEvidenceA short quotation, embedded in your sentence. He “hurls” the poker into the fireplace.
EExplainZoom in on one word and its connotations. Use because to justify. “Hurl” has violent connotations, because it means to throw with great force.
LLinkUse tentative language for the writer’s purpose: may, could, perhaps. Conan Doyle may be hinting that Roylott was behind Julia’s death.

Model paragraph: How is Dr Roylott presented?

Dr Roylott is presented as violent and aggressive when he says “See that you keep yourself out of my grip” and leaves “hurling the twisted poker into the fireplace”. The verb “hurling” has vicious connotations, because it means throwing something with great force. In particular, the word “grip” suggests that he wants to control and hurt people. This could suggest that Roylott has something to hide. Ultimately, Conan Doyle may be hinting that Roylott had something to do with his stepdaughter’s death.

Model paragraph: How is Helen Stoner presented?

Conan Doyle presents Helen Stoner as terrified and vulnerable when she arrives “with restless frightened eyes, like those of some hunted animal”. The simile compares her to defenceless prey, because a hunted animal cannot escape. In particular, the word “hunted” suggests that someone is targeting her. Perhaps Conan Doyle does this to make the reader worry that she is in danger, and to build suspense.

Checklist: a clear idea, an embedded quotation, one word zoomed in on, “because” to justify, and tentative language (may, could, perhaps) for why Conan Doyle wrote it.
